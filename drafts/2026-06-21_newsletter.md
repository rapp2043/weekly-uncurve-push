SUBJECT LINE: The Half-Billion-Dollar Mistake: What Los Angeles Learned from the Yacht Tax

On a sweltering afternoon in July 2023, a real estate developer named Michael—he asked that his last name not be used, because he still has to work in this city—stood on a dusty lot in Koreatown, holding a set of architectural plans that had cost him nearly two million dollars. The plans were for a thirty-two-unit condominium building, all but six of which would sell for well over five million dollars. He had spent three years navigating permits, environmental reviews, and community meetings. He had the financing lined up. The only thing that changed, he told a reporter from the commercial real estate publication *The Real Deal*, was a single line in the city tax code: Measure ULA, also known as the “mansion tax.” It added a 4 percent charge on any property sale above five million dollars and 5.5 percent on sales above ten million. For Michael’s project, that meant a tax burden of roughly six hundred thousand dollars per unit. He did the math. Then he did something that would surprise no one who understands the economics of luxury goods: he stopped everything. “I’m not building a monument to Los Angeles’s tax policy,” he said. “I’m trying to build a building that makes sense.” He packed up the plans and walked away.

Why would a city, desperate for affordable housing, pass a law that makes housing more expensive to build? That is the great riddle of Los Angeles’s Measure ULA. The tax, approved by voters in November 2022 with nearly 58 percent support, was designed to raise an estimated $600 million to $1 billion annually for affordable housing and homelessness services. The logic seemed perfect: tax the rich, help the poor, build homes for those who need them. It was righteous. It was simple. It was, as it turns out, the kind of policy that looks good on a ballot measure but falls apart when it meets reality.

In the first year after the tax took effect on April 1, 2023, luxury home sales in Los Angeles collapsed. According to data from the real estate brokerage Douglas Elliman, the number of transactions for properties over five million dollars in the city dropped by more than 50 percent compared to the year before. In some months, the drop was even steeper. Sellers were not eager to give up millions to the city. But the problem was even deeper. Developers, like Michael, who had planned new luxury projects—the very projects that would have generated the tax revenue the city was counting on—simply stopped building. A study by the UCLA Ziman Center for Real Estate, published in early 2024, estimated that new development applications for projects over $5 million had fallen by nearly 70 percent since the tax took effect. The city’s budget office, which had projected $250 million in tax revenue for the first fiscal year, received only about $150 million. The affordable housing fund, the intended beneficiary, was short by tens of millions of dollars.

But here’s the thing about unintended consequences: they almost never come out of nowhere. The history of well-meaning taxes that backfire is long and well documented. And the most instructive case—the one every city should study before passing a luxury tax—took place three decades ago, on the opposite coast.

In 1990, as part of a broad deficit-reduction package, the United States Congress imposed a 10 percent luxury tax on the sale of aircraft, boats, jewelry, furs, and cars priced above $30,000. The target, as with the LA mansion tax, was the wealthy. It was a way to pay for tax cuts for the middle class, and it had bipartisan support. The reasoning was simple: rich people buying expensive toys wouldn’t change their behavior just because of a small surcharge. The tax would raise about $9 billion over five years, the government estimated. But Congress had not consulted the people who actually build boats and planes.

Consider the case of Chris-Craft Industries, a storied boat manufacturer based in Sarasota, Florida. In 1990, the company employed 2,500 workers. By 1991, orders had fallen by 50 percent. The tax made a new $1 million yacht more than $100,000 more expensive. The rich, it turned out, are not stupid. They bought used yachts, or they bought boats made in France or Italy, or they simply waited. One broker told a congressional hearing that he had a client who wanted a new $500,000 yacht but was now “not going to pay an extra $50,000 in tax—they’ll buy a boat that’s a year old.” The luxury tax created a crash in the American boat-building industry. Twenty thousand jobs were lost in the first year alone. In 1991, the United States Customs Service recorded that the number of yachts imported into the country actually rose while domestic production collapsed. The tax was raising far less than projected—only about $20 million a year instead of the $900 million expected from boats alone. And then something remarkable happened: the government spent millions of dollars on unemployment benefits for the laid-off workers. A study by the Joint Committee on Taxation later concluded that the luxury tax was a net loser for the federal budget. It cost more in economic damage than it generated in revenue. In 1993, Congress repealed the luxury tax on boats, and on jewelry, and on aircraft. The yachts came back.
