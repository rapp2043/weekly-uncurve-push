SUBJECT LINE: The Anti-Smoking Ad That Made Teens Start Vaping

When the ad came on, seventeen-year-old Diana watched without flinching. It was the one with the tracheostomy hole—a woman in her fifties, her voice a mechanical whisper through the stoma, explaining that she used to think cigarettes made her look cool. The camera lingered on the hole in her throat as she coughed. Diana had seen it before, dozens of times. It played during YouTube breaks, between TikTok videos, on the bus shelter across from her high school in suburban Atlanta. She knew what it was supposed to do: scare her so badly that she would never touch a cigarette. But that’s not what happened. What happened was that Diana started watching videos about vaping. “I figured they were just trying to make smoking look scary,” she told a reporter in 2023. “But vaping isn’t smoking. It seemed different. Safer.” Within six months, she was using a Juul—the same device her school’s anti-drug assembly had warned her about, but that the graphic ads had somehow made her curious about. Diana is not an anomaly. She is part of a strange, stubborn pattern that public health experts are only beginning to understand: well-intentioned fear campaigns can produce the opposite of their intended effect.

That pattern is not a simple one. It doesn’t work by some single mechanism. Instead, it operates through at least three distinct paradoxes, each one a separate way that graphic anti-smoking ads can quietly, invisibly, nudge a person toward the very behavior they are designed to prevent. To understand these paradoxes is to understand something deeper about how the human mind responds to fear—and why the most intense warnings sometimes create the most intense curiosity.

Let’s start with the first paradox, the one that likely drove Diana’s transformation. Let’s call it the Forbidden Fruit Effect. For decades, psychologists have known that warning labels can increase desire for a product. In a classic experiment from the 1970s, researchers told children that a particular toy was forbidden. The children immediately wanted to play with that toy more than the others. The same logic applies to the adult brain when it encounters a frightening image paired with a forbidden substance. A 2023 study published in *Tobacco Control* examined how adolescents reacted to graphic warning labels on cigarette packs. The researchers found that among teens who were already prone to risk-taking, the graphic images actually increased their curiosity about smoking. The fear was there, but it was overridden by a stronger impulse: the desire to defy authority. Anti-smoking ads, by being so aggressively scary, become a dare. They tell a teenager: *This is so dangerous, you must never do it.* And a subset of teenagers hears: *This is so dangerous, only the brave will try.* That is the first paradox: the more terrifying the warning, the more alluring the risk.

The second paradox is quieter, and it takes longer to see. Let’s call it the Desensitization Effect. In 2021, a team of researchers at the University of Pennsylvania led by Dr. Emily Brennan published a paper in *Tobacco Control* that tracked how smokers and nonsmokers responded to repeated exposure to graphic anti-smoking images. The results were unsettling. People who saw the images frequently—on cigarette packs, on billboards, in online ads—gradually stopped feeling any emotional response to them. The images that once made them recoil became background noise. And when the fear wears off, what is left? The image itself remains. The smoker’s lungs, the blackened teeth, the hole in the throat. But now it is stripped of its emotional jolt. It becomes just a picture. And a picture of a diseased lung, seen for the hundredth time, can start to look almost...normal. Several studies have shown that heavy exposure to fear-based health warnings can backfire by making the behavior seem less dangerous, not more. The logic is perverse but clear: if seeing a picture of a diseased lung doesn’t make you flinch anymore, you begin to assume the disease is not as bad as you once thought. This is the second paradox: the cure for fear is familiarity, and familiarity breeds indifference.

The third paradox is perhaps the most insidious because it does not involve a direct reversal of intention. It involves a substitution. Let’s call it the Misattribution Effect. In the early 2010s, as the United States ramped up its graphic anti-smoking campaigns, a new product emerged: e-cigarettes. They were marketed as a safer alternative, a way to quit. But what the anti-smoking ads did, inadvertently, was create a powerful contrast. If smoking was represented as a lung-destroying, throat-rotting horror, then vaping suddenly looked like something else entirely. It looked clean. It looked modern. It looked like a rational choice. A 2019 study published in *JAMA Pediatrics* surveyed teenagers and found that those who had seen the most graphic anti-smoking ads were also the most likely to believe that vaping was harmless. The logic was simple: if smoking is a monster, then anything less monstrous must be safe. The ads had established such an extreme baseline of disgust that any alternative, no matter how harmful, appeared benign by comparison. That is the third paradox: the more you demonize one behavior, the more you legitimize another that is only *less* bad.

Now, take these three paradoxes together and you begin to see the shape of the problem. A well-funded, well-intentioned public health campaign does not act in a vacuum. It enters a complex ecosystem of human psychology, social rebellion, and market innovation. The anti-smoking ads of the past two decades have undoubtedly saved millions of lives. But they have also, in ways that are difficult to measure, created the conditions for the vaping epidemic. Because the same teenagers who ignored the warnings were drawn to the forbidden; the same adults who stopped feeling fear became numb to the images; and the same public that was taught to see cigarettes as uniquely evil found themselves open to a product that was merely dangerous.

This is not a simple story of failure. It is a story of unintended consequences, and it raises a question that public health officials are only starting to grapple with: what happens when the most powerful tool in your arsenal—fear—turns against you?

There is a historical parallel here that is worth considering. In the 1980s, the United States launched the “Just Say No” campaign against drugs. It was a simple, fear-based message: drugs are bad, don’t use them. By many measures, it failed. It did not reduce drug use. In fact, some research suggests it may have increased curiosity among teenagers. The parallel is not perfect, but the mechanism is the same. When you make something simply forbidden and surround it with images of horror, you create a kind of magnetic repulsion that, for a certain kind of mind, acts as an attraction. The anti-smoking campaigns were smarter; they used real medical images, not just slogans. But they may have fallen into a similar trap: they made smoking so terrifying that anything else seemed sane.

So where does that leave us? Let’s return to Diana, the seventeen-year-old from Atlanta. After six months of vaping, she developed a chronic cough. Her doctor told her she had the lungs of a smoker. When she saw the graphic anti-smoking ads again, she felt something different. Not fear. Not curiosity. She felt a kind of hollow recognition. The ads had failed her. But they had also helped create the very market that hooked her. She quit vaping eventually, but it took two years and three attempts. The ads that were supposed to protect her had, instead, sent her down a different path. “I wish they had just told me the truth,” she said. “Not that smoking would kill me, but that vaping might, too.” That is the lesson, perhaps. Fear alone is not a sufficient warning. It needs nuance. It needs context. It needs to account for the strange ways the human mind can turn a threat into an invitation. And it needs to remember that the most dangerous thing you can do with a powerful message is to assume it will always work exactly as intended.

[^1]: The forbidden fruit effect is a well-documented phenomenon in psychology. In one of the most famous demonstrations, researchers told children not to play with a specific toy. The children immediately wanted that toy more. The effect is stronger in adolescents because their brains are wired for novelty and risk-taking.

[^2]: The desensitization effect has been observed in many fields, from violence in media to climate change warnings. The more often you see an image of a polar bear on a melting ice floe, the less likely you are to feel urgency about global warming. The brain treats repeated exposure as a signal that the threat is not imminent.

[^3]: The JAMA Pediatrics study I referenced was led by Dr. Jessica Pepper and colleagues. She is now a researcher at the University of North Carolina's Tobacco Prevention and Evaluation Program. The study found that among adolescents, exposure to graphic anti-smoking ads was associated with increased curiosity about e-cigarettes.

[^4]: It is worth noting that not all public health experts agree with this analysis. Some argue that the benefits of graphic warnings far outweigh any unintended consequences. But even they acknowledge that the vaping epidemic requires a different, more nuanced messaging strategy.
