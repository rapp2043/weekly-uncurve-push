SUBJECT LINE: The Surprising Science of Losing Weight Fast

Sometime in late 2014, Joseph Proietto sat in his office at the University of Melbourne, staring at a spreadsheet that didn’t make any sense. He’d spent the better part of three years running a trial that compared two approaches to weight loss. On one side, a group of patients had followed a very low‑energy diet—roughly 500 to 800 calories a day—designed to drop pounds fast. On the other side, a group had followed the standard, gradual approach: about 1–2 pounds a week, achieved through modest calorie reduction. The conventional wisdom was clear: slow and steady wins the race. But the spreadsheet told a different story. At the end of the study, the rapid‑loss group had not only lost more weight, they had kept it off longer. “I didn’t believe it at first,” Proietto later told a reporter. “We had to recheck the data twice.”

Before I tell you what that recheck revealed, let’s sit with the assumption that Proietto’s data was about to shatter. For decades, the message from every diet book, every doctor, every public‑health campaign has been the same: lose weight slowly. Crash diets are dangerous. They slow your metabolism, make you lose muscle, and guarantee that you’ll gain everything back. The body, the theory goes, interprets rapid starvation as a famine and fights back with a vengeance. So we have been trained to aim for a modest, sustainable deficit. One or two pounds a week. Anything faster is reckless.

But here’s the problem: that cherished piece of wisdom—so deeply embedded in our culture that it feels like a biological law—turns out to be much less grounded in evidence than any of us ever imagined. And the story of how it became a dogma, and how one researcher’s spreadsheet started to unravel it, is a perfect example of what the sociologist Murray Davis called the “order is chaos” pattern. We think the system is stable, when in fact it is built on a wobbly foundation.

To understand where the “slow and steady” rule came from, we have to go back to the early 1970s, to a time when rapid weight loss was not just accepted but celebrated. Remember the “Last Chance Diet”? In 1971, a book of that title promoted a liquid‑protein fast that promised lightning‑fast results. Thousands of people tried it. Some died. By the time the public‑health establishment finished investigating the deaths—cardiovascular complications linked to the diet—the pendulum had swung hard in the opposite direction. The medical community issued warnings: rapid weight loss was dangerous. The American Medical Association published guidelines calling for no more than two pounds of loss per week. And that became the rule.

Support for this rule came from a series of studies in the 1970s and 80s that seemed to show that people who lost weight quickly regained it faster. One classic experiment by Dr. George Bray at Louisiana State University—a towering figure in obesity research—found that the body’s resting energy expenditure dropped sharply after a rapid loss, creating a “starvation response” that made it easy to pack the weight back on. That finding was the key. Once Bray’s data entered the textbooks, the idea of slow loss became orthodoxy. Every weight‑loss program after that was built on the assumption that you must trick the body into losing fat without triggering its ancient famine defenses.

But a funny thing happened on the way to the clinic. Over the last decade, a handful of researchers began to question whether Bray’s experiment really applied to the modern patient. The “starvation response” had been studied mostly in people who were already lean, or in extreme metabolic ward conditions. What happened when you put a group of overweight or obese individuals on a very low‑energy diet for a short period—and then carefully transitioned them to a maintenance plan? That was exactly the question Proietto and his colleagues set out to answer.

Their study, published in *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* in 2014, followed 200 obese adults for three years. Half were assigned to a rapid weight‑loss program: 12 weeks on a strict very‑low‑energy diet, followed by a gradual reintroduction of food. The other half followed a conventional slow‑loss program: 36 weeks of moderate calorie restriction, aiming for the same total weight loss. At the end of the weight‑loss phase, both groups had lost the same amount—about 15 percent of their body weight. That part wasn’t surprising. The surprise came later.

After one year, the rapid‑loss group had regained less weight than the slow‑loss group. After three years, they were still ahead—an average of 72 percent of the weight remained off, compared to just 53 percent in the slow‑loss group.[^1] Proietto’s spreadsheet was right. The rapid diet not only worked, it appeared to be more effective in the long run.

How could that be? The theory is counterintuitive, but elegant. When you lose weight slowly, the body has plenty of time to adapt—to lower your metabolic rate and increase hunger hormones like ghrelin. That adaptation can happen gradually and persistently. With rapid weight loss, the body may experience a bigger initial shock, but the adaptation appears to be shorter‑lived. By the time you finish the rapid phase and start eating normally again, the metabolic machinery hasn’t had time to lock into a new, lower baseline. It’s a strange idea: maybe the best way to outsmart the body’s famine response is to get the job done before the body figures out what’s happening.

Here is a small thought experiment to illustrate the point. Imagine you have two runners. One runs a marathon in three hours—a slow, steady pace. The other runs it in two and a half hours—faster, but with a shorter total exertion time. Which one’s muscles are more likely to adapt and become efficient over many races? In running, the slow, steady runner builds endurance and becomes more economical. But in weight loss, that economy is precisely what you don’t want. You want the body to *not* adapt, to keep its metabolism humming. And rapid loss, by being over quickly, may prevent the body from settling into a new, thrifty equilibrium.

That is the modern puzzle. But why did it take so long for someone to see it? Because the dogma against rapid loss was so strong that researchers rarely, if ever, tested it head‑to‑head against the gold standard. Proietto told an interviewer that his grant application was rejected twice because reviewers were convinced the rapid diet would be dangerous. “They thought it was unethical,” he said. The study was only funded after he agreed to an intensive medical monitoring protocol.

The lesson here is not that you should start a crash diet tomorrow. The rapid‑loss diet in the study was a medically supervised, nutrient‑complete program—not a cabbage‑soup fast. The danger zone is unsupervised fad diets, which still carry real risk. But the deeper insight is about how easily a single plausible theory—combined with a dramatic, well‑publicized disaster—can become an unshakeable rule.
